# IA Criteria Digest — IB DP Sciences

Teacher-authored digest in the author's own words, distilled from the official Biology guide and TSM — **the four-criteria IA model is identical across Biology, Chemistry, and Physics** (Research design / Data analysis / Conclusion / Evaluation, 6 marks each, 24 total; the Chemistry and Physics guides carry the same descriptors). Facts and structure; paraphrase, not reproduction. **The official guide remains the authority for exact descriptor wording** — when marking, cross-check against the teacher's copy if available.

## Assessment structure (facts)

- One task: the **scientific investigation**. 10 hours of teaching time, 20% of final grade, same at SL and HL.
- Report max **3,000 words**. Not counted toward the limit: charts/diagrams, data tables, equations/formulas/calculations, citations/references, bibliography, headers.
- Report opens with: title, candidate code, group member codes (if any), word count. No cover page or contents page required.
- **Four criteria, 6 marks each, total 24**: Research design, Data analysis, Conclusion, Evaluation.
- Marking: best-fit against level descriptors; whole numbers only; credit what is present; each criterion judged on its own (no assumed mark distribution).
- Approaches allowed: hands-on lab work, fieldwork, spreadsheet analysis/modelling, database extraction, simulation.
- Every student has a unique research question; no two students present the same raw data.
- Collaborative work optional, groups ≤ 3: different IV, or same IV with different DV, or different data from a communal set; **all authoring is individual** (methodology description included). A school-built class database counts as a database investigation (methodology = how data was filtered/sampled).
- Referencing expected; omitted or sloppy referencing = academic malpractice. Broad facts need no citation; specific facts (e.g. "alpha-amylases have an optimum pH of 6.8") do.

## Research design — 6 marks

How clearly the report lays out **what was done, why, and how** — the method, its purpose, and the way it was carried out.

The three strands, by markband (1–2 → 3–4 → 5–6):

1. **Research question with context**: the question stands alone → it is sketched into a general background → it sits inside a tightly relevant setting. That setting names the two measured quantities (or the two correlated variables), identifies the actual system being investigated, and brings in only the background theory that bears directly on it.
2. **Methodological considerations**: listed → outlined → explained with reasoning. Covers: how the IV and DV are measured; for database/model work, why that source and how the data were sampled; the scope, amount and quality decisions (the span of the IV, its spacing or sampling frequency, how many repeats, how precisely); control variables and how each is held steady; and the safety, ethical and environmental issues that were considered.
3. **Methodology description**: detailed enough to reproduce — named materials, exact steps, and nothing irrelevant or duplicated.

Teacher-check notes (from the TSM): the question is the student's own; an IV is present (or two correlated variables); the DV may be derived; everything is measurable; scientific name where relevant; background stays on the exact question (a general survey never reaches the top band); the protocol records what was actually done including the trialling (not a plan); standard protocols are cited; sampling rate is fixed by the trialling; confounding variables are labelled as *monitored* when they cannot be controlled; a trial run is described; the risk assessment includes mitigations (naming a hazard is not handling it); with no safety/ethics/environmental concerns, the student says so outright; fieldwork includes the site; database work includes screenshots with URLs and an explained filtering/sampling procedure.

## Data analysis — 6 marks

Whether the data were **captured, worked up, and displayed in forms that speak to the question**. (Deciding what the data mean against the question is Conclusion's job.)

1. **Communication of recording and processing**: muddled in both clarity and convention → readable or convention-correct but not both → both easy to follow and convention-correct. Clear = the processing is easy to follow; correct = the conventions are met (graph/table labelling, units, decimal places, significant figures).
2. **Consideration of uncertainties**: barely touched → handled but with notable gaps or mistakes → handled properly.
3. **Processing relevant to the RQ**: large gaps, errors or contradictions → a few notable gaps → accurate and appropriate. (Large problems stop a valid conclusion; notable ones limit how far or how precisely it can go.)

Key conventions (TSM): SI or metric units only (imperial or kitchen measures not acceptable); decimal places kept consistent with the measurement precision (significant-figure rules are not compulsory, but correct when used); a mean / SD / range shown at the foot of a data column already counts as processing; software is fine if formulas are shown via screenshot; a statistical test needs its null and alternative pair stated (not necessarily up front) and the software named; raw and processed data may sit in one table; a representative sample of a large dataset is acceptable; a value the device itself computes (an instrument that outputs "rate") is raw data; a graph produced by software is raw data; a gradient or an area-under-curve may extend the processing.

## Conclusion — 6 marks

How well the report **answers its question in light of both its own data and the wider scientific record**.

1. **Answering the RQ**: a claim the data don't support → a claim that only partly squares with the data → an argument that is warranted and matches the data — and "matches" means the processed numbers *and their uncertainties* are read and interpreted.
2. **Scientific context**: a passing nod to outside sources → a real but shallow comparison → a comparison that is argued against traceable published material, published values, course notes or textbooks.

Teacher-check notes: explain trends validly; answer the question in its setting; don't bend the interpretation to a preferred answer; interpret the statistical test (p < 0.05 is the usual significance line); discuss what the uncertainties mean for how trustworthy the data are; admit when the sample is small; use "optimum", "maximum", "intercept" where they apply; inconclusive data are fine if handled honestly; where no accepted value exists (some ecology work), argue whether the result is sensible.

## Evaluation — 6 marks

How well the report **appraises its own method and proposes fixes**.

1. **Weaknesses/limitations**: vague faults that apply to any experiment → named, specific faults → the actual effect of each specific fault, ranked. Weaknesses concern control of variables, measurement precision, or scatter in the data; limitations concern scope (the range covered, the boundaries of the system, whether the assumptions hold).
2. **Improvements**: plausible fixes named → fixes that clearly address the faults named → fixes whose reasoning is spelled out and matched to each fault. Fixes must be doable in a school/field setting; a generic "run more trials" or "use finer instruments" only counts when it is tied to a specific shortcoming.

Teacher-check notes: weaknesses exclude careless-manipulation errors and imagined problems with no evidence; an experiment always has limitations even run perfectly (a simulation can show few weaknesses while still carrying real limitations); judge impact qualitatively — error propagation is not expected; systematic errors (an uncalibrated instrument, a consistent human tendency) shift accuracy while random variation spreads precision; measuring can disturb the system (a cold thermometer cools the sample, an observer changes animal behaviour); for database work, don't re-audit the sources (that's Research design) but reflecting on how the data were curated adds value.

## Command terms in the descriptors (depth ladder)

AO1: state. AO2: identify, outline, describe. AO3: explain, justify. Higher bands demand the deeper terms.

## Tools and skills relevant to investigations

- **Tool 1 — Experimental techniques**: safety; measuring mass/volume/time/temperature/length; observations (counts, annotated diagrams, qualitative notes, classifying); chromatography, colorimetry/spectrophotometry, serial dilutions, microscopy + eyepiece graticule, temporary mounts, identification/classification, random and systematic sampling, karyograms, cladogram analysis.
- **Tool 2 — Technology**: sensors; databases; models/simulations; spreadsheets; graphical representation; computer modelling; image analysis.
- **Tool 3 — Mathematics**: mean/median/mode; range, SD, SE, IQR; rates of change; percentage change/difference; chi-squared, t-test, Simpson reciprocal index, Lincoln index; uncertainties recorded as ranges (±); error bars from ranges/SD/SE; R² for trend-line fit; correlation coefficient r.
- **Inquiry process**: exploring & designing → collecting & processing data → concluding & evaluating.

## Legacy note — the older syllabus (older cohorts)

The older-syllabus scheme had **five** criteria: Personal engagement (2), Exploration (6), Analysis (6), Evaluation (6), Communication (4) — total 24. Personal engagement rewarded the student's interest/initiative; Exploration covered design (RQ, variables, methodology, safety); Analysis covered data processing and presentation; Evaluation covered conclusion + evaluation (combined); Communication covered structure and clarity. Confirm the cohort before marking; the current four-criterion scheme is this skill's default.

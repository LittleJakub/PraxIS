# Session Subject Reports — Examiner Feedback Across the Sciences

Distilled into the author's own words from the official session subject reports (Biology, Chemistry, Physics — the first sessions under the current syllabus, several timezones, both the opening and the following session). Use in Mode C as corroboration for the calibration gates, and in Mode B to shape review comments.

## Common across the sciences (both sessions)

- **Moderation runs strict.** Examiners repeatedly report internal marks sitting higher than the evidence supports — simulated and home-based work especially gets marked down after moderation even when the teacher commented positively, and generous marks rarely survive closer inspection.
- **Teacher marking is the main lever.** Many submissions lack the per-criterion notes that let a moderator see the reasoning; large cohorts are inconsistently marked unless internally standardised; comments that only praise mislead; old-scheme habits linger (a full page for the retired Personal Engagement criterion; docking a strand for something no longer required, such as a "strengths or extensions" heading in Evaluation); pasting descriptors in without a best-fit argument does nothing for anyone.
- **Anonymity slips repeatedly.** Real names and school identifiers keep showing up on work that should carry only a candidate code.
- **Length ≠ quality.** Submissions of 25–43 pages that claim to be 3,000 words are common; oversized tables and images are just as awkward to moderate as tiny ones — and since tables and figures sit outside the word limit, shrinking them has no justification.
- **Topic choice.** Confirmatory topics with known outcomes, brand or location comparisons, and recycled procedures dominate. Examiners still want creativity: students who pursue a genuinely open problem write stronger Conclusion and Evaluation sections because they are not leaning on a template.
- **Front page basics.** The word count and candidate code go on page one; a clear title and page numbers help the moderator.

## Chemistry (opening session, all timezones)

- **The "5 × 5" rule is conditional.** Five repeats and a standard deviation apply only when a student sets out to fit a trend with SD — SD is not itself a requirement, and concordant values are an accepted alternative. SD needs at least five readings. Statistical tests (ANOVA and similar, routine in Biology) are not expected on chemistry-sized datasets, and some teachers wrongly demanded them.
- **Processing errors.** Rate is not 1/time; rate is not the rate constant (activation-energy work); averaging logarithms is wrong; show the initial and final readings, not only the derived volume or temperature change; report what was measured, not what was hoped for (degradation studies must actually chill the sample and titrate at controlled temperatures).
- **Uncertainty muddle.** Percentage uncertainty, percentage error and percentage difference are routinely confused (many treat percentage difference as purely systematic). Error bars are optional in chemistry, but their basis must be stated — wide or overlapping bars let almost any trendline through. The uncertainty list has to match the raw data; don't tack tiny uncertainties onto large ones.
- **Fitting misbehaviour.** Stretching a line or deleting points to push R² up is data tampering; R² is misread (a value like 0.3 is not "moderately strong", and a high R² with overlapping bars still needs a defence); trendlines on categorical variables; raw data plotted where processed data belongs.
- **Conclusion.** Described rather than argued; teachers lenient when the conclusion rests on thin processed data; outlier and anomaly treated as synonyms; jumping from a dish to a whole organism without caveats; confirming the hypothesis by forcing the processing.
- **Context comparison (strand 2).** Often omitted, or repeats the design section (which caps the band); cited studies must resemble the actual conditions; "supported by many references" with nothing specific earns nothing; references themselves are not marked by any criterion, but padded bibliographies and student-written sites are noticed.
- **Appendices are not read.** Tables and processing hidden there are lost; only consent forms belong in an appendix.
- **Evaluation.** Still the weakest section — lists of limits and fixes with no discussion, generic or imagined weaknesses, fixes for conditions never described. Teachers over-reward long lists of flaws that the design stage should have caught; strengths and "future work" are optional and must not move the mark.

## Physics (both sessions, all timezones)

- **Rounding is a real expectation.** No calculation improves precision: sums and differences keep the fewest decimal places, products and quotients the fewest significant figures; absurd precision (an efficiency quoted to ten decimals with an uncertainty to nine) is flagged.
- **Least count as the floor.** When repeated readings don't move, the instrument's least count is the uncertainty.
- **Don't re-derive the Data Booklet.** Describe and use standard equations. When the question restates known physics, present it as testing the known relationship (predicting the outcome is fine; a hypothesis is unnecessary for a relationship that is already established) — don't act as if it is undiscovered.
- **Fits and gradients.** Never force a straight line onto curved scatter (residuals are the honest test of linearity); never draw max/min gradient lines from just the two extreme points (students ended up with gradients in both directions, which empties the conclusion of meaning); if a gradient and its uncertainty are quoted, say what they imply.
- **Outliers.** Rejecting a reading needs a reason beyond "it fits better"; at most one rejection — when a reading looks suspect, remeasure instead.
- **Statistics vs precision.** Running a statistical test while ignoring much larger precision-limited uncertainties is a common error.
- **Conclusion.** Argue the physics, not the statistics. Strand 2 asks for a check against the known world (a genuine reference — local values of g, not a universal constant); with no accepted standard, a plausible value or range will do (comparing a spring constant to similar springs).
- **Evaluation.** The hardest criterion: standard random/systematic/human-error catalogues are never tied to this analysis. One significant flaw with one well-matched fix can reach the top; insight beats a long list of vague complaints; strengths earn nothing and are not expected. Conclusion and Evaluation together are worth half the IA mark.
- **Questions and scope.** Clever or contrived questions get tangled; two independent variables cause trouble; even the simplest question scores when the student shows depth and the required skills.
- **Authenticity.** Many reports are visibly copied from online material (theory, method and write-up near-identical to the online original). AI-generated content carries no authority.

## Biology (both sessions; second-session specifics)

- **Hypotheses.** Where given, a hypothesis should be an explanation, not a null/alternative pair, unless a specific statistical test requires the latter.
- **The question should carry the IV range.** Data-based work should state the collection period; field work should state the site and season.
- **Background.** Explain the expected link from IV to DV using properly cited scholarly sources (textbooks, peer-reviewed articles) rather than general websites.
- **Controlled variables.** Lists with no note on how each variable is held steady or monitored.
- **Protocols.** Name the bacterial source and confirm a non-pathogenic strain; incubate at sensible temperatures.
- **Old PE habits.** A topic-rationale written in a thin "personal engagement" style.
- **Simulations.** Often too shallow, with little student choice or meaningful processing.

# Session Subject Reports — Examiner Feedback Across the Sciences

Paraphrased in the author's own words from the official session subject reports (Biology, Chemistry, and Physics; the first sessions under the current syllabus — multiple timezones, both the first and second sessions). These are the examiner's own words on what candidates and teachers did well and badly. Use in Mode C (marking support) as corroboration for the gates, and in Mode B (review) to steer comments.

## Common across the sciences (both sessions)

- **Moderation still runs strict**: examiners repeatedly note teachers awarding marks higher than the evidence justifies — including simulation/home-based work "substantially downgraded" despite enthusiastic teacher comments, and work where teachers' generous marks "did not resist closer scrutiny".
- **Teacher marking practice is the leverage point**: many schools omit the detailed per-criterion comments that let moderators understand the marks; large cohorts show inconsistent marking (internal moderation needed); praise-only comments are misleading; some teachers still apply old-curriculum habits (a whole page on Personal Engagement; penalizing strands for things no longer required — e.g. "strengths or extensions" in Evaluation); copying the descriptors without showing best-fit reasoning helps no one.
- **Anonymity keeps slipping**: student names and school identifiers still appear on submitted work. Reports must carry only the candidate code.
- **"More is not better"**: reports of 25–43 pages claiming 3,000 words are common; oversized tables and images are as hard to moderate as tiny ones; graphs/tables are excluded from the word count so there is no excuse for shrinking them.
- **Topic choice**: verification-type topics with well-known outcomes, brand/location comparisons, and recycled well-worn methods dominate; examiners still expect innovation and creativity — and students who pursue genuinely open investigations write richer Conclusions and Evaluations (they avoid clichéd solutions).
- **Word count and ID** must be on the first page; a descriptive title and page numbers help.

## Chemistry (first session, TZ1–TZ3)

- **The "5 × 5" n-rule is conditional**: five repeats and standard deviation only apply when the student decides to establish a trend with SD — SD is *not* a requirement; concordant values are a legitimate alternative. SD requires ≥ 5 values; statistical tests (ANOVA etc., common in biology) are not expected on chemistry-sized datasets — several teachers wrongly expected them.
- **Processing misconceptions**: rate is not the reciprocal of time; rate ≠ rate constant (Ea investigations); do not average logarithmic values; show the raw (initial/final) readings, not only the processed volume/∆T; report experimental values, not desired ones (temperature-degradation studies must actually chill samples and titrate at consistent temperatures).
- **Uncertainty currency**: % uncertainty vs % error vs % difference are routinely confused (many think % difference is purely systematic error); error bars are *not required* in chemistry, but if used their origin must be explained — long/overlapping error bars admit too many trendlines; uncertainty lists must match the raw data; don't add negligible uncertainties onto large ones.
- **Graphs and fits**: forcing lines or deleting data to raise R² is manipulation; R² is misread (values like 0.3 called "moderately strong" — there is no ideal value, and high R² with overlapping error bars needs discussion); trendlines on non-continuous independent variables; raw data plotted instead of processed.
- **Conclusion**: described rather than justified; teachers generous when the conclusion rests on limited processed data; outliers vs anomalies confusion; in-vitro → physiological extrapolation without caveats; hypotheses confirmed by forcing the processing.
- **C2 (context comparison)**: often skipped or repeats RD1 (caps at middle band); reference studies must match the investigation's conditions; vague "supported by many references" adds nothing; citations are not assessed by any criterion — but bibliographies "beefed up" with unrelated articles and student sites are noted.
- **Appendices are not read** — data tables and processing placed there are lost (consent forms are the only permissible appendix).
- **Evaluation**: still the weakest section; lists of limitations+improvements without discussion; generic/hypothetical weaknesses; improvements for conditions never reported; teachers award generously for long lists of design-stage limitations that should have been addressed during the design; strengths and "further investigations" are not requirements and must not affect marks.

## Physics (both sessions, TZ1–TZ3)

- **Rounding laws are examinable expectations**: no calculation improves precision — addition/subtraction results keep the least number of decimal places; multiplication/division results keep the least number of significant figures; meaningless sig figs (e.g. an efficiency of 0.0787750624% ± 0.560080827%) are flagged.
- **Least count, not zero**: when repeated measurements do not vary, the minimum uncertainty is the least count.
- **Don't derive what's in the Data Booklet** — describe and use well-known equations; when the RQ restates known physics, frame it as confirming the known function (an expectation of the result is appropriate; no hypothesis is needed for a known relationship); don't pretend the relationship is unknown.
- **Fits and gradients**: do not force a linear line on non-linear scatter (residuals can justify/reject a linear model); never use only the first and last data points for min/max gradient lines (students claimed both positive and negative gradients, making conclusions meaningless); if a gradient and its uncertainty are quoted, interpret what they mean.
- **Outliers**: rejecting a datum requires justification (not "looks better"); at most one datum should be rejected — remeasure instead when a datum is in question.
- **Statistics vs precision**: running statistical analysis while ignoring much larger precision-based uncertainties is a common fault.
- **Conclusion**: focus on the physics, not the statistical meaning; strand 2 needs comparison to the known world (a reliable source — e.g. local g values, not a universal 9.81); with no accepted standard, a plausible value or range suffices (e.g. comparing a spring constant to similar springs).
- **Evaluation**: still the hardest criterion — textbook random/systematic/human-error lists are not connected to the actual analysis; a single major weakness with an appropriate improvement can earn top marks; insight beats a long list of generic issues; strengths are neither relevant nor required. Conclusion + Evaluation together are half the IA marks.
- **Questions and scope**: clever or contrived research questions get muddled; two independent variables cause trouble; even the simplest RQ earns marks when the student shows depth and the required skills.
- **Authenticity**: many investigations are visibly copied from online material (nearly identical theory, method and analysis). AI-generated content is not authoritative.

## Biology (both sessions; second-session specifics)

- **Hypotheses**: where provided, hypotheses should be explanatory — not framed as null or alternative hypotheses unless required for statistical testing.
- **The research question should specify the range of the independent variable**; data-based investigations should specify the data-collection time period; field investigations should specify location and time of year.
- **Background**: justify the relationship between the independent and dependent variables using correctly cited academic sources (textbooks, peer-reviewed literature) rather than general internet sites.
- **Controlled variables**: lists without an explanation of how each will be monitored or maintained.
- **Protocols**: identify the source of bacteria and confirm non-pathogenic strains; incubate at appropriate temperatures.
- **PE leftovers**: rationales for topic choice written in a superficial "personal engagement" style.
- **Simulations**: often too simplistic, with little student choice or meaningful processing.

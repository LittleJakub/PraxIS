# Marking Calibration — Lessons from a Real Moderation Case

Anonymized case study: a 2026 May-session moderation of six DP Biology IAs (5 HL, 1 SL) from one school. Teacher's internal marks totalled 99/144; the moderator's marks totalled 80/144 (**−19 marks, every single report moderated down**). Two reports dropped two component grade levels (e.g. grade 7 → grade 5). The IB's own feedback described the marking as broadly consistent and appropriate while noting some overestimation in two reports — yet the aggregate gap was large. The lesson: **consistent, plausible-sounding marking can still run systematically 2–3 marks per report too generous.** This reference exists to stop that.

Use this in Mode C (mark) as a calibration gate before finalizing any mark, and in Mode B (review) to keep feedback honest.

## The pattern: what was over-credited

Across the six moderated-down reports, the same gaps recurred — and the teacher's 5s and 6s had been awarded despite them:

1. **No significance test / no p-value.** The moderator repeatedly flagged reports where no p-value or significance test appeared, where statistical outputs were not presented, and where claims of statistical significance were made without any formal calculation. The official samples confirm: no significance testing caps the Data analysis processing strand at 2–3.
2. **Uncertainties absent or unused.** Measurement uncertainties not included; a turbidity dataset with no uncertainty or variability measure; uncertainty treatment described as limited. Uncertainties must appear (column headings, error bars) AND be interpreted in the conclusion — otherwise Data analysis ≤ 4 and Conclusion ≤ 5.
3. **Inconsistent units and conventions.** Conflicting units across tables (e.g. µg/cm³ vs µg/m³; seconds vs minutes) — the precision strand is damaged wherever this appears.
4. **Reproducibility gaps.** Spectrophotometer calibration not stated, room temperature left undefined, a species mismatch between materials and method, initial measurements mislabelled. Research design strand 3 (reproducibility) caps at 3–4.
5. **Unjustified choices.** An explicit justification missing for a chosen data period; a 30-minute cut-off needing further explanation. Range/interval/period choices without rationale cap Research design strand 2.
6. **Conclusion praised but not justified.** Conclusions that answered the research question and matched the processed data were still capped at 4 — the 5–6 band needs interpretation of processed data **including uncertainties** and comparison to scientific context with quantitative depth, not generality.
7. **Evaluation improvements without impact or implementation.** Relative impact not fully explained; improvements needing further implementation detail — listing weaknesses + realistic improvements = 3–4; explaining relative impact + how each improvement would work = 5–6.

## The Hedge Test (the single most useful rule)

**If your own marking justification contains a hedge — "mostly", "generally", "somewhat", "largely", "with potential", "would benefit from", "could be strengthened", "not fully", "limited" — the criterion is a 3–4, not a 5–6.** Every report in the case study carried hedges in the teacher's justification while receiving 5–6 marks. Top bands require the full descriptor met without significant reservations.

Operational rule: write the justification first, then read it. If any hedge appears, drop the mark to the lower band and re-justify. A 5–6 justification should contain no "would strengthen" suggestions.

## Calibration gates (apply in order before finalizing any mark)

| Gate | If... | Then the criterion is capped at |
|---|---|---|
| **Uncertainty presence** | No uncertainties stated (no ±, no column-heading uncertainties, no error bars) | Data analysis ≤ 3–4 |
| **Significance test** | No test / no p-value where a relationship or difference is claimed | Data analysis processing strand 2–3 (criterion ≈ 3–4) |
| **Uncertainty interpretation** | Conclusion never interprets uncertainty/error against the RQ | Conclusion ≤ 4–5 (never 6) |
| **Reproducibility** | Another researcher could not repeat it (unit errors, missing calibration, undefined terms, species/material mismatch) | Research design ≤ 4 |
| **Justification of choices** | IV range/interval/period/stat test chosen without rationale | Research design strand 2 ≤ 3–4 |
| **Impact in evaluation** | Weaknesses/limitations listed without explaining relative impact; improvements not tied to them | Evaluation ≤ 4 |
| **Hedge test** | Your own justification contains hedges | Drop to lower band |

## What "appropriate" marking looked like in the case

The moderator's comments rewarded exactly what the official samples reward: units consistent and in column headings, uncertainties present and interpreted, a significance test actually run and its output reported, choices justified, methods reproducible, evaluation explaining impact. Reports that had these kept their teacher's mark; reports that only gestured at them lost 2–6 marks.

## Calibration habit for teachers

- Mark a report **cold** (read fully, write justifications), then apply the gates and the hedge test before writing the final mark.
- Keep a moderation history: after each session, compare your marks to the moderated marks and tally the gap per criterion — the pattern (e.g. "I always over-credit Conclusion") becomes visible and fixable.
- When in doubt between two bands, **choose the lower band** — the case study's errors were all in the generous direction, and moderation only ever moved marks down.

## Session context — the first session under the current syllabus, from the official subject report

The examiner feedback for the first session under the four-criteria scheme (paraphrased from the first-session Biology subject report) explains why moderation ran strict:

- **"Standard" experiments dominated** — enzyme activity, osmosis, seed germination, photosynthesis. Ecology, database, and simulation approaches were notably rare. With Personal Engagement gone as a criterion, many students reverted to basic foundational investigations — which the report notes **limits the justification students can offer in Research design** and **limits the Conclusion's comparison to accepted scientific context**. Innovation and creativity are still expected.
- **Protocol slips persisted** (e.g. incubating bacteria at temperatures meant for pathogens; not stating that cultures were non-pathogenic and sourced). These cost marks in Research design's safety/ethics strand.
- **Simulations were often too simplistic** — little scope for student choice or meaningful processing.
- **Data-based IAs' greatest weakness was variable choice**: broad non-biological metrics such as the Human Development Index produce data that is hard to analyse well. Variables need to be specific and biological.
- **The evaluation of data-based work was weak** — the reliability of the data was rarely assessed.
- **Many teachers left out the per-criterion comments that back up their own marks** — and the moderator explicitly calls these comments valuable. (This is a direct, actionable fix: write criterion-referenced comments on every report you mark; it protects both your students and your moderation.)
- **Official IA component grade boundaries** (from the session grade-boundaries publication; identical for Biology, Chemistry, and Physics, HL and SL, all timezones): 7 = 20–24, 6 = 17–19, 5 = 14–16, 4 = 11–13, 3 = 7–10, 2 = 4–6, 1 = 0–3. (Session overall subject boundaries are published per session and vary slightly by subject/timezone; the IA component table above has been uniform across the sciences.)

**Anecdotal corroboration from student-reported moderation outcomes (first new-syllabus sessions):** multiple independent reports across schools describe the same pattern as the case study — teacher marks of 19–23 moderated down to 14–15; a teacher-predicted 6 dropping to an IB component 3; schools that "never see drops" getting hit; one school capping internal raw marks at 19 for moderation safety; a confirmed 21/24 HL Biology IA (yeast fermentation vs temperature). Treat these as directional (self-reported, unverified), but they align with the case study and the subject report: first-session moderation ran strict, and internal marking ran generous.

*Provenance note:* the criterion profiles in the tables below aggregate a corpus of real student IAs with confirmed component grades collected from teacher-accessible sources, cross-checked against the official assessed samples. Corpus scores are internally consistent (criterion sums match reported totals) but are indicative, not official — the official grade boundaries remain authoritative.

**Do not split calibration by level (HL/SL).** The corpus shows HL averaging roughly two marks higher than SL in every subject (Biology 15.5 vs 13.4, Chemistry 16.4 vs 14.9, Physics 17.9 vs 15.9), and the gap is spread across all four criteria. This is a cohort-strength effect (HL students are, on average, stronger students), not a different marking standard — the criteria, descriptors, and component boundaries are identical at both levels per the subject guides. Applying a level-adjusted mark would bake in a bias (marking an SL report down "because SL averages lower") and make the outcome *less* accurate, not more. Mark every report against the same descriptors regardless of level.

Implication for marking: a "safe standard" investigation (enzyme/osmosis/germination) with good execution is a solid 4–5; reaching 6 in Research design or Conclusion in this session required genuine depth of justification and scientific context — the thing the report says standard experiments limit. ### What a 6/6 actually looks like (patterns from current-rubric exemplars)

- **Research design 6/6**: IV and DV named in the RQ; IV range chosen to span the expected relationship (e.g. pH levels straddling the optimum so the conclusion can discuss shape); controls listed **with tolerances**; the statistical test justified *before* data collection. Capped at 5/6 when the hypothesis wasn't fixed before accessing data, or when the biological rationale is thin.
- **Data analysis 6/6**: ≥5–6 replicates per level with individual values, means, SD; worked calculation shown; model fit reported (R²); error bars drawn with ±SE (correctly, for mean comparisons).
- **Conclusion 6/6**: states direction **and magnitude** (e.g. "glucose gave 2.3× the rate of sucrose", with confidence intervals); links to the mechanism (enzymes involved); cites literature values and compares; explicitly does not over-claim.
- **Evaluation 6/6**: weaknesses **ranked by impact** (largest → smallest); random vs systematic errors separated; improvements are specific and equipment-level ("replace beaker with a thermostatically controlled chamber", not "use better equipment"); extensions test the mechanism (e.g. KCl at matched osmotic potential to separate ion-specific from osmotic effects).
- **Evaluation 3/6** (the shape of over-marked work): generic weaknesses ("human error") with no impact analysis; improvements like "do more trials" with no specific change; no random/systematic distinction; a clear confound missed (e.g. caffeine habituation). If a report's evaluation looks like this, it is not a 5.

Patterns worth internalizing:

What the merged data settles:

- **All four criteria scale monotonically with grade** (RD 3.2→5.4, DA 1.8→4.5, C 2.0→5.0, E 2.0→4.8 across grades 3–7). No single criterion decides everything — but there are dependable gates.
- **B (Data analysis) is the strongest gate at the bottom**: grade 3 reports average B 1.8 — B ≤ 2 almost never coexists with a grade above 3. If your B is 3, the grade is 4–5; if B is 4+, the grade is 5–6.
- **A (Research design) is the strongest gate at the top**: A averages 4.3 at grade 5 but 5.0 at grade 6 and 5.4 at grade 7. An RD that reads 3–4 cannot be a 6, however strong the analysis — check RD first when a report feels like a 6.
- **C and E each contribute ~0.5–0.6 marks of separation per grade** — the 5-vs-6 decision is genuinely spread across all four criteria; don't try to settle it on one.
- **Trust the summed criteria, not the reported grade label**: 19 of 220 reported grade labels (8.6%) deviate from the official boundary implied by their criterion sum (a grade-6-labelled IA with 12/24, a grade-7-labelled IA with 16/24). Sum A+B+C+D, then map to boundaries: ≤10 → 3, 11–13 → 4, 14–16 → 5, 17–19 → 6, ≥20 → 7.
- **Collapsed criteria cap reports**: G6 with B1, G4 with B0, G3 with RD1 — one empty strand drags the best-fit mark down hard. Mark the weakest strand honestly.

Subject notes:

- **The same gates hold**: DA ≤ 2 implies grade ≤ 3; RD 5 is the near-necessary condition for 6+ in all three subjects.
- **Physics 7s run higher per criterion than Chemistry 7s** (DA 5.3 vs 4.9, total 20.9 vs 20.6) — and Physics 3s average E 3.0 (unusually high for the band, small n). Do not assume one subject's boundary behaviour transfers mark-for-mark; keep subject-tagged calibration.
- **The boundary map is identical** for all sciences: sum ≤10 → 3, 11–13 → 4, 14–16 → 5, 17–19 → 6, ≥20 → 7.
- Chemistry 3s show a distinctive E crash (E 1.8, C 1.5) — weak chemistry reports often end abruptly after data processing; check the Conclusion/Evaluation are present and substantive before awarding mid-bands.
- Physics 3–4 show the highest DA relative to other criteria (DA 2.2–3.3 vs C 2.3–2.9) — physics students process data competently but fail to interpret or evaluate; a physics report with solid DA but thin C/E is a 4, not a 5.

**What the examiner commentary most often penalizes** (mined from 518 weakness notes across 48 IAs, ranked):

1. **The effect of each method flaw on the final numbers is never ranked or explained** — the single most common gap (~1 in 5 reports). Listing weaknesses without ranking how each one shifted the results caps Evaluation at 3–4.
2. **Conclusion does not compare to the accepted scientific context** — citing literature without a quantitative or explicit comparison caps Conclusion at 4.
3. **Uncertainties listed but not propagated or used** — stated in the method, absent from the analysis and conclusion.
4. **The conclusion claims more than the data can support** (over-claiming from correlational data).
5. **Improvements named but never reasoned through** (tied to a specific weakness with implementation detail).
6. Reproducibility nits that cost Research design: replicate counts unjustified, time/volume inconsistencies between the variables table and the procedure, calibration steps ambiguous, taxonomic typos.

These mirror the official samples and the moderation case exactly — they are the dependable mark-loss points to check for in every report you grade.
